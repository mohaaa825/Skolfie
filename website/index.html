How it works

Several detectors. One clear answer.

Skolfie doesn't rely on a single trick. It combines different ways of reading a text and weighs them into one calibrated score.

Writing style

About 130 measurable traits of the text: sentence length and how much it varies, word choice, punctuation, function words and vocabulary richness. Classic stylometry, including Burrows' Delta, the method used to identify authors of disputed texts.

Language model

A DeBERTa transformer fine-tuned on hundreds of thousands of human and AI texts, including fresh essays written by today's models next to real student essays on the same topics.

Predictability

AI text tends to choose the most likely next word. Skolfie compares how surprising each word is to two open language models, a signal that keeps working on new chatbots.

Mixed texts

Long texts are checked piece by piece, so a student essay with a pasted-in AI paragraph is labelled Mixed and the AI-looking passages are highlighted. (This part is being retrained for the newest AI models.)

Reasons you can read

Every verdict comes with plain-language reasons, such as "unusually even sentence lengths", and warnings when a text is too short or the result is less reliable.

Responsible use

A starting point for a conversation.

No AI detector is proof. Skolfie is built to support a teacher's judgment, never to replace it.

  • Use it alongside what you know. Drafts, notes and how a student talks about their work matter more than any score.
  • Longer is better. Texts under 150 words get a warning, and under 30 words get no verdict.
  • Language learners. Writing by students still learning English is flagged more often. Skolfie shows a warning on those results.
  • English only, for now. Other languages get a warning instead of a confident verdict.
